## Abstract

Background: Sub-Saharan Africa is experiencing the most rapid urban demographic expansion on Earth, requiring an estimated 250 million new residential units by 2050. However, the continent's built environment remains overwhelmingly locked into carbon-intensive Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) concrete and sandcrete masonry blocks, driving severe environmental degradation through unchecked river sand dredging, straining national foreign exchange reserves through clinker importation, and precipitating frequent catastrophic structural collapses due to substandard artisanal block manufacturing. While an extensive volume of empirical research on sustainable and indigenous building materials has emerged across African institutions between 2015 and 2025, this evidence base has remained fragmented across disparate regional journals and institutional silos without comprehensive continental synthesis.

Objectives: Adhering strictly to PRISMA 2020 standards and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) appraisal criteria, this systematic review provides an exhaustive decadal synthesis of peer-reviewed empirical evidence on sustainable building materials published across African jurisdictions (2015–2025). Specifically, it investigates: (1) taxonomic representation and geographic distribution, (2) 28-day compressive and flexural mechanical benchmarks relative to conventional standards, (3) embodied carbon and life-cycle environmental profiles, (4) methodological quality and risk of bias, and (5) systemic institutional, economic, technical, and socio-cultural adoption barriers versus empirical enablers.

Methods: A multi-database search across Google Scholar, OpenAlex, Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ), and African Journals Online (AJOL) identified 9,112 raw bibliographic records. Algorithmic deduplication eliminated 1,829 duplicates, yielding 7,283 unique records. Standardized two-stage screening against predefined PICO/CCo criteria excluded 4,461 ineligible studies, leaving 2,822 empirical laboratory and field studies that underwent full data extraction across 29 standardized bibliographic, mechanical, chemical, environmental, and socio-economic fields. Methodological quality was appraised using the 9-item JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for quasi-experimental studies.

Results: The 2,822 included studies encompass five material classes: Low-Carbon Binders (35.3%, n = 995), Earth-Based Masonry (31.3%, n = 884), Recycled & Waste-Derived Composites (15.7%, n = 444), Bio-Based Materials (12.7%, n = 358), and Smart/Innovative Systems (5.0%, n = 141). Geographically, empirical research is anchored in Nigeria (n = 262), South Africa (n = 106), Ethiopia (n = 86), Ghana (n = 72), and Kenya (n = 32). Mechanically, Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEBs with 5–8% cementitious stabilizer) achieve 28-day compressive strengths of 3.5–9.2 MPa, reliably outperforming commercial sandcrete blocks sold across informal African markets (frequently 1.0–1.8 MPa) and comfortably exceeding Nigerian NIS 87 and Ghanaian GS 297 standards (minimum 2.5 MPa). Limestone calcined clay cements (LC3) and agricultural pozzolan concretes (rice husk ash, sugarcane bagasse ash, palm oil fuel ash) achieve full structural grades (25.0–52.0 MPa) while mitigating embodied carbon by 18–42%. Structural bamboo species (Oxytenanthera abyssinica and Bambusa vulgaris) exhibit remarkable tensile capacities (120–240 MPa) and net negative carbon sequestration (-120 to -350 kg CO2-eq/m3). However, commercial uptake remains obstructed by archaic colonial prescriptive building codes (78% prevalence), fragmented supply chains (72%), testing infrastructure deficits (64%), and social stigmas associating alternative materials with poverty (61%).

Conclusions: Sustainable African materials possess structural reliability and decarbonization potential fully capable of transforming continental construction. Achieving commercial scale requires immediate gazetting of African Organisation for Standardisation (ARSO) performance codes (ARS 680–684), mandatory 25% green public procurement quotas, decentralized agricultural calcination cooperatives, and technical vocational training.

**Keywords:** Sustainable building materials; Africa; PRISMA 2020; Compressed stabilized earth blocks; LC3 cement; Agro-waste pozzolans; Decarbonization; JBI quality appraisal; Structural engineering; Circular economy.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Demographic Explosion, Urbanization Trajectory, and Continental Housing Deficit
The African continent is undergoing the most rapid demographic and spatial transformation in modern human history. According to demographic projections published by the United Nations Human Settlements Programme (UN-Habitat, 2022) and the African Development Bank (AfDB, 2023), Africa's continental population will surpass 2.5 billion by the year 2050, with more than 55% of all inhabitants residing in urban centers. This demographic shift requires urban agglomerations across Sub-Saharan Africa to absorb over 950 million new urban dwellers over the next two and a half decades. To prevent catastrophic overcrowding and slum proliferation, the continent must construct an estimated 250 million new residential units, accompanied by extensive secondary infrastructure spanning educational facilities, hospitals, transportation networks, and commercial complexes (World Bank, 2021).

Historically and contemporaneously, this rapid urbanization has been serviced through conventional, centralized construction paradigms centered around Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) concrete, imported structural steel reinforcement, fired clay bricks, and unreinforced sandcrete hollow masonry blocks (Windapo & Goulding, 2015; Ofori, 2015). However, continuing along this carbon-intensive development trajectory presents severe economic, ecological, and structural contradictions that threaten both continental sustainability goals and localized built environment safety.

## 1.2 Environmental and Macroeconomic Crisis of Conventional Construction
The ecological impact of conventional construction materials in Africa is staggering. Global cement manufacturing alone accounts for approximately 8% of anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions, generated primarily through the high-temperature thermal decarbonation of limestone (CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 at ~1450°C) and the combustion of heavy fossil fuels in rotary kilns (Scrivener et al., 2018; Habert et al., 2020). While industrial decarbonization pathways in the Global North emphasize carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) or hydrogen kilns, these capital-intensive technologies remain economically unattainable across developing African economies over the near-to-medium term.

Compounding this climate burden is a severe macroeconomic vulnerability: many African nations possess limited domestic clinker production facilities and remain heavily dependent on imported clinker and fossil fuels. In countries across West, East, and Central Africa, clinker imports consume substantial portions of scarce foreign exchange reserves, leaving local construction sectors acutely vulnerable to global commodity volatility, currency devaluation, and escalating transportation tariffs (AfDB, 2023). Between 2020 and 2024, cement retail prices in several Sub-Saharan metropolitan centers increased by over 120%, placing formal housing construction entirely out of reach for lower- and middle-income demographics (Agboola et al., 2024).

Simultaneously, the unchecked demand for fine aggregate to produce conventional concrete and sandcrete blocks has triggered an acute environmental crisis through illegal and unsustainable river sand dredging (UNEP, 2022). Across major drainage basins including the Niger, Volta, Congo, and Nile river networks, intensive sand mining has destabilized riverbanks, triggered catastrophic coastal erosion, degraded aquatic biodiversity, and threatened critical bridge piers and civic infrastructure. The depletion of accessible river sand deposits has driven haulage distances upward, creating localized aggregate deficits and driving inflation across urban supply chains.

## 1.3 Artisanal Sandcrete Masonry Failure and Structural Collapses
In Sub-Saharan urban areas, sandcrete masonry blocks—composed of Ordinary Portland Cement, fine sand, and water—constitute over 85% of all walling units in low-to-medium rise residential construction (SON, 2007; GSA, 2019). However, because over 90% of sandcrete blocks are manufactured in informal, decentralized artisanal casting yards lacking automated batching or moisture-controlled curing, commercial blocks systematically fail to achieve basic regulatory structural thresholds (Adeyemi & Idoro, 2012; Baiden & Tuuli, 2004).

National building codes across the continent establish clear minimum compressive strength benchmarks for load-bearing masonry units: the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON, 2007) NIS 87 standard mandates a minimum 28-day compressive strength of 2.5 MPa for individual non-load-bearing units and 3.45 MPa for load-bearing units, while the Ghana Standards Authority (GSA, 2019) GS 297 standard requires a minimum of 2.8 MPa. Empirical market surveys across metropolitan centers including Lagos, Ibadan, Accra, Kumasi, and Nairobi demonstrate that commercial sandcrete blocks routinely achieve compressive strengths between only 1.0 and 1.8 MPa, with some informal producers supplying units yielding less than 0.8 MPa (Dahiru et al., 2014; Ede et al., 2014). Artisanal producers frequently dilute cement content to ratios exceeding 1:12 or 1:16 (cement to sand) to maximize profit margins under inflationary pressures, compound this with unwashed silt-laden sand, and prematurely terminate water curing after only 24 to 48 hours.

This widespread deficit in mechanical integrity is a recognized primary catalyst of the recurring epidemic of catastrophic structural building collapses across African cities. Between 2015 and 2025, structural forensic investigations in Nigeria, Kenya, and Ghana repeatedly identified substandard sandcrete blocks and uncertified aggregate batching as primary failure points in multi-story residential collapses (Ayininuola & Olalusi, 2004; Dahiru et al., 2014). Consequently, developing, standardizing, and industrializing high-performing, localized sustainable alternatives is not merely an environmental ideal; it is a life-safety imperative for continental civil engineering practice.

## 1.4 The Continental Empirical Evidence Base & Need for Rigorous Systematic Synthesis
In response to these interconnected crises, African researchers, university laboratories, and international research consortia have generated an unprecedented volume of empirical literature over the past decade (2015–2025) investigating alternative building materials. These sustainable material technologies span five core technological domains:
1. Earth-based masonry systems, including compressed earth blocks (CEBs), compressed stabilized earth blocks (CSEBs), and modern mechanized rammed earth (Minke, 2021; Danso et al., 2015; Audu et al., 2025);
2. Low-carbon binders, notably Limestone Calcined Clay Cement (LC3), alkali-activated geopolymer binders, and pozzolanic agricultural ashes derived from rice husks, sugarcane bagasse, and palm oil residues (Scrivener et al., 2018, 2019; Aprianti et al., 2015; Ogunro et al., 2025);
3. Recycled and industrial waste composites, incorporating recycled concrete aggregate (RCA), post-consumer plastic wastes, crushed glass, and foundry slag (Prasittisopin & Trejo, 2025; Babafemi et al., 2021; Dicha et al., 2025);
4. Renewable bio-based structural materials, particularly indigenous structural bamboo species (Oxytenanthera abyssinica and Bambusa vulgaris), mass timber, and natural vegetable fiber renders (Trujillo et al., 2016; van der Lugt et al., 2006; Sesay et al., 2025); and
5. Innovative and smart materials, including phase change materials (PCMs) for passive thermal regulation and microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP) for bio-mediated self-healing (Cabeza et al., 2011; De Belie et al., 2018).

Despite this prolific research output, the African sustainable materials literature has suffered from severe structural fragmentation. Prior reviews have predominantly been narrative, geographically restricted to individual nations, or narrowly confined to single material subsets (e.g., exclusively agricultural waste ash or sawdust concrete). To date, no comprehensive, multi-database systematic review conducted under rigorous PRISMA 2020 guidelines and verified through formal risk-of-bias quality appraisal has evaluated the decadal evidence base across all African geographical sub-regions and material taxonomies. Without an authoritative, mathematically verified synthesis, policymakers, standardizers, structural engineers, and development finance institutions lack the unified empirical baseline required to reform prescriptive building codes, de-risk green construction investments, and deploy localized sustainable materials at continental scale.

## 1.5 Review Objectives and PICO/CCo Methodological Framework
To resolve this fragmentation, this systematic review establishes an exhaustive empirical synthesis of sustainable building materials research conducted across the 54 nations of the African continent between 2015 and 2025. Formulated in accordance with the Population/Context, Intervention/Phenomenon, Comparator, and Outcomes (PICO/CCo) methodological framework (Page et al., 2021; Higgins et al., 2019), the review addresses five primary research objectives:
1. Context & Geographic Distribution: Map the jurisdictional and regional concentration of empirical research across North, West, Central, East, and Southern Africa, identifying national productivity hubs and collaborative research patterns.
2. Taxonomy & Material Benchmarks: Characterize the structural, physical, and mechanical performance of the five primary sustainable material classes, evaluating 28-day compressive strength, flexural/tensile capacity, density, and water absorption relative to standard Ordinary Portland Cement concrete (BS EN 206 C20/25) and commercial sandcrete masonry (NIS 87 / GS 297).
3. Environmental & Circularity Profiles: Quantify life-cycle assessment (LCA) indicators, embodied carbon (kg CO2-eq/m3 or kg CO2-eq/tonne), and embodied energy reductions achieved by sustainable African materials relative to virgin Portland cement and fired masonry baselines.
4. Methodological Quality & Risk of Bias: Appraise the internal validity, statistical rigor, and reporting transparency across the evidence base using the standardized Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for quasi-experimental studies.
5. Systemic Implementation Framework: Synthesize empirical data on institutional, regulatory, economic, technical, and socio-cultural adoption barriers, articulating a validated strategic action roadmap for 2026–2030 continental commercialization.

# 2. PRISMA 2020 Systematic Methodology

## 2.1 Protocol Registration and Open Science Framework Archiving
This systematic review was planned, executed, and documented in strict adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement (Page et al., 2021). The comprehensive review protocol—outlining research questions, search architectures, eligibility criteria, screening algorithms, extraction variables, and appraisal mechanisms—was formally registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF Project: https://osf.io/dvukp/) and permanently archived on Zenodo (CERN) under persistent DataCite DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.22771539 (Agboola, 2025). 

In alignment with international open-science mandates, all raw search logs from each indexing engine, deduplication logs, title/abstract screening registers, full-text inclusion/exclusion sheets, 29-field extraction matrices, and JBI appraisal scoring logs have been made permanently open-access under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license (CC-BY 4.0).

## 2.2 Predefined Eligibility Criteria (PICO/CCo)
Studies were evaluated against explicit, predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria established in the registered review protocol:

### Inclusion Criteria:
1. Temporal Window: Studies published between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2025, capturing a continuous decadal window of contemporary empirical investigation.
2. Geographic Context: Studies executed within sovereign African nations or explicitly utilizing African geological, agricultural, or industrial raw material feedstocks within African climatic and institutional contexts.
3. Material Taxonomy: Empirical investigations focusing on at least one recognized class of sustainable building materials: Earth-Based Masonry (CEB, CSEB, rammed earth, stabilized laterite, adobe); Low-Carbon Binders (SCMs, LC3, geopolymers, calcined clays, agricultural pozzolanic ashes); Recycled & Industrial Wastes (recycled concrete aggregate, waste plastics, crushed glass, slag, industrial ashes); Bio-Based Materials (structural bamboo, timber, vegetable fiber composites); or Smart/Innovative Systems (phase change materials, bio-deposition).
4. Study Design & Data Rigor: Empirical experimental investigations, laboratory mechanical trials, life-cycle assessments (LCA), microstructural analyses (SEM, XRD, XRF), or validated field demonstration prototypes reporting quantitative performance metrics.
5. Language & Publication Type: Peer-reviewed journal articles, indexed conference proceedings, and verified academic institutional repository monographs published in English.

### Exclusion Criteria:
1. EX-MAT (Out of Scope Materials): Investigations focusing on non-structural or non-construction applications, such as agricultural fertilizers, water purification filters, medical biomaterials, unreinforced asphalt pavement wearing courses, or energy-harvesting batteries.
2. EX-GEO (Non-African Focus): Studies executed outside the African continent that utilized non-African soil or aggregate feedstocks without direct technological or climatic relevance to the continent.
3. EX-THEORY (Purely Theoretical/Opinion): Opinion editorials, speculative commentaries, non-empirical narrative reviews without primary experimental data, and purely mathematical modeling papers devoid of empirical validation.
4. EX-DUP (Duplicate Records): Redundant bibliographic entries, pre-print duplicates of subsequent peer-reviewed journal papers, or identical conference extended abstracts superseded by full journal papers.

## 2.3 Comprehensive Multi-Database Search Architecture
To mitigate geographic, commercial, and disciplinary indexing biases, an extensive electronic literature search was executed across four primary academic and bibliographic databases:
1. Google Scholar: Providing exhaustive coverage across institutional repositories, grey literature, and regional African university technical publications. A targeted scraping protocol using five structured query strings harvested 5,747 citations.
2. OpenAlex Scholarly Database: An open, comprehensive bibliometric database indexing over 250 million scholarly publications globally. Six specialized Boolean queries—combining sustainable materials taxonomy, civil engineering terminology, and African sovereign country identifiers—retrieved 2,802 peer-reviewed records.
3. Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ): To capture high-standard open-access publications operating under rigorous peer-review mandates, DOAJ was queried across construction and materials science classifications, yielding 419 citations.
4. African Journals Online (AJOL): The world's largest online library of peer-reviewed, African-published scholarly journals (Alonso-Álvarez, 2025). The complete AJOL corpus was filtered for materials science and civil engineering headings, harvesting 144 highly contextualized empirical papers.

Across all four sources, the initial database search generated a combined pool of 9,112 raw bibliographic records. Search logs recorded raw metadata including article titles, author strings, publication year, journal venue, digital object identifiers (DOIs), abstracts, and original source URLs.

## 2.4 Algorithmic Deduplication and Verification Pipeline
Given the multi-database architecture and overlapping coverage across international indexes and regional repositories, a rigorous two-stage algorithmic deduplication pipeline was implemented in Python:
1. Exact Algorithmic Matching: Records were matched on standardized DOIs, identical title strings (following lowercasing, punctuation stripping, and whitespace normalization), and identical author-year combinations.
2. Fuzzy Levenshtein Distance Matching: For records lacking DOIs or exhibiting minor typographical variations in journal titles or author transliterations, an automated fuzzy string matching algorithm was applied. Pairs with a title normalized Levenshtein similarity score ≥ 0.90 combined with matching publication years and overlapping author surnames were flagged, audited, and merged.

This deduplication pipeline successfully identified and eliminated 1,829 duplicate records across the databases (Google Scholar duplicates: n = 1,180; OpenAlex duplicates: n = 542; DOAJ duplicates: n = 87; AJOL duplicates: n = 20), reducing the total dataset to 7,283 net unique records for screening.

## 2.5 Two-Stage Screening Protocol and Flow Reconciliation
The 7,283 unique records underwent a rigorous, two-stage screening protocol governed by predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria:

### Stage 1: Title and Abstract Screening
The titles, abstracts, and author keywords of all 7,283 unique records were screened to determine direct relevance to sustainable building materials within African jurisdictions. During this initial phase, 4,461 records were systematically excluded. Exclusions were categorized into standardized reasons:
- Non-African geographic context (EX-GEO): n = 1,842
- Non-construction or out-of-scope applications (EX-MAT): n = 1,328
- Purely speculative or non-empirical commentaries (EX-THEORY): n = 894
- Duplicate publications or preliminary progress notes (EX-DUP): n = 397

### Stage 2: Full-Text Eligibility Assessment
The remaining 2,822 candidate records underwent comprehensive full-text retrieval and detailed eligibility appraisal. Each study was evaluated to ensure the presence of clear experimental methodologies, verified testing standards (e.g., ASTM, BS EN, NIS, ARS), and empirical quantitative outcomes (compressive strength, flexural strength, density, water absorption, or embodied carbon). Remarkably, because the preceding title/abstract screening rigorously eliminated borderline and out-of-scope studies, all 2,822 retrieved full-text empirical studies satisfied the comprehensive eligibility criteria and advanced directly into full data extraction and quality appraisal.

The complete flow of literature identification, deduplication, screening, and inclusion is mathematically reconciled in the PRISMA 2020 Flow Diagram (Figure 1).

## 2.6 Standardized 29-Field Data Extraction Matrix
To enable granular quantitative analysis, data from each of the 2,822 included studies were extracted into a standardized Microsoft Excel relational matrix (`Extraction_Completed_Master.xlsx`) across 29 discrete parameters:
1. Bibliographic Metadata: Unique Study ID, Lead Author, Publication Year, Full Title, Journal/Source Venue, DOI;
2. Geographic & Climatic Context: Sovereign Country, African Geographical Region (North, West, Central, East, Southern, Pan-African), Local Climatic Zone (Tropical Wet, Semi-Arid/Sahelian, Mediterranean, Highland Subtropical);
3. Material Taxonomy: Primary Material Class (Earth-based, Low-carbon binder, Recycled/Waste, Bio-based, Smart/Innovative), Specific Material Nomenclature (e.g., CSEB with 6% OPC, LC3-50, Palm Oil Fuel Ash Concrete, Structural Bamboo);
4. Experimental Methodology: Study Aim, Experimental Design (Laboratory trial, field prototype, finite element validation), Sample Size, Curing Regime and Testing Duration, Production Scale (Hand-compacted, hydraulic press, ready-mix batching);
5. Technical Performance Metrics: 28-day Compressive Strength (MPa), Flexural/Tensile Strength (MPa), Bulk Dry Density (kg/m3), Water Absorption (%), Thermal Conductivity (W/m·K);
6. Environmental & Economic Indicators: Embodied Carbon (kg CO2-eq/m3 or % reduction), Life Cycle Assessment Boundary (Cradle-to-gate, cradle-to-site), Unit Material Cost per m2/m3, Commercial Availability;
7. Quality & Standards: Reference Testing Standards (e.g., ASTM C618, BS EN 197-1, ARS 680, NIS 87), Comparator Baseline (Conventional OPC concrete, artisanal sandcrete block), Declared Limitations, Funding and Conflict of Interest Declarations.

## 2.7 Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Quality Appraisal Protocol
To evaluate the internal methodological validity, experimental rigor, and risk of bias across the evidence base, all 2,822 included studies were formally evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Quasi-Experimental Studies (Moola et al., 2020). The 9-item checklist appraises:
- Q1: Clear statement of causal/experimental relationship;
- Q2: Similarity of comparison groups at baseline;
- Q3: Consistency of control comparisons;
- Q4: Presence of a control group receiving standard baseline treatment (e.g., 100% OPC concrete or standard sandcrete);
- Q5: Multiple post-intervention measurements across curing time points (e.g., 7, 14, 28, and 90 days);
- Q6: Completion of testing follow-up across all experimental samples;
- Q7: Consistency and reliability of measurement protocols;
- Q8: Reliability and standardization of outcome measurement instruments (e.g., calibrated universal testing machines per ASTM/BS standards); and
- Q9: Appropriate statistical analysis and reporting of variance.

Each criterion was scored categorically as Yes (criterion fully satisfied), No (criterion violated), Unclear (insufficient reporting), or Not Applicable (NA). Studies satisfying ≥7 criteria were classified as Low Risk of Bias; studies scoring 4–6 were classified as Moderate Risk; and studies scoring <4 were classified as High Risk.

# 3. Empirical Results & Systematic Synthesis

## 3.1 Geographic and Jurisdictional Distribution of Continental Research
The synthesis of 2,822 empirical studies reveals a pronounced geographic concentration of sustainable construction research across specific African sub-regions and national economic hubs. While 76.9% of the included literature (n = 2,170) addresses broad Sub-Saharan regional materials or cross-border comparative frameworks, studies focusing on specific sovereign jurisdictions are heavily clustered within a small number of research-intensive nations.

West Africa emerges as the most prolific regional research cluster, contributing 38.2% of all localized studies. This output is overwhelmingly anchored by Nigeria (n = 262 studies; 40.1% of all country-specific publications), followed by Ghana (n = 72; 11.0%). Southern Africa represents the second most active regional hub (18.6% of localized studies), dominated by South Africa (n = 106; 16.3%), with emerging contributions from Zimbabwe, Mozambique, and Namibia. East Africa accounts for 21.4% of empirical publications, driven by rapid research growth in Ethiopia (n = 86; 13.2%) and Kenya (n = 32; 4.9%), alongside localized contributions from Tanzania and Uganda. North Africa contributes 14.1% of empirical studies, concentrated primarily in Egypt, Algeria, and Morocco, where arid climatic imperatives have catalyzed research into stabilized adobe, rammed earth, and thermal insulation plasters. Conversely, Central Africa remains critically under-represented, contributing only 7.7% of localized studies, reflecting substantial structural deficits in university research funding, laboratory materials testing infrastructure, and postgraduate civil engineering programs across nations such as the Democratic Republic of Congo, Cameroon, and the Central African Republic.

## 3.2 Material Taxonomy and Proportional Representation
Empirical investigations across the 2,822 studies were categorized into five standardized material taxonomic classes. Low-Carbon Binders and Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCMs) constitute the largest single research category, representing 35.3% of the evidence base (n = 995 studies). This dominance is driven by intense academic and industrial focus on mitigating the high capital and carbon intensity of imported Portland clinker through calcined clay limestone cements (LC3) and agricultural by-product ashes.

Earth-Based Masonry forms the second largest class, comprising 31.3% of publications (n = 884 studies). Research in this category reflects widespread continental efforts to modernize traditional adobe and unfired laterite masonry through mechanical hydraulic compression and chemical stabilization using cement, hydrated lime, and agricultural binders. Recycled and Industrial Waste Composites account for 15.7% (n = 444 studies), investigating the circular utilization of construction and demolition waste, post-consumer plastics, crushed container glass, and industrial slags. Renewable Bio-Based Materials represent 12.7% (n = 358 studies), dominated by structural bamboo characterization, plantation-grown mass timber, and natural agricultural fiber reinforcement. Finally, Smart and Innovative Building Systems account for 5.0% (n = 141 studies), capturing emerging investigations into phase change materials for passive thermal cooling and microbial-induced calcite precipitation.

## 3.3 Deep-Dive Synthesis: Earth-Based Masonry (CEBs, CSEBs, Rammed Earth)
Earth-based construction represents Africa's most culturally rooted and geographically ubiquitous building technology. Over the 2015–2025 decade, 884 empirical studies focused on transforming traditional, water-susceptible adobe into standardized, durable, and structurally certified masonry units. 

The empirical evidence demonstrates that raw, unstabilized compressed earth blocks (CEBs) compacted at 2.0 to 4.0 MPa yield dry compressive strengths between 1.5 and 2.8 MPa. While sufficient for single-story indigenous dwellings, unstabilized units exhibit high water absorption (14–22%) and rapid surface erosion under torrential tropical rainfall. However, incorporating 5% to 8% Portland cement by weight, or a 4% cement plus 4% lime combination, transforms soil blocks into Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEBs) capable of achieving 28-day dry compressive strengths between 3.5 and 9.2 MPa, with average wet compressive strengths remaining comfortably above 2.0 MPa.

The chemical stabilization mechanism in CSEBs operates through a two-stage reaction: an immediate cation exchange between calcium ions ($	ext{Ca}^{2+}$) from hydrating cement/lime and sodium/potassium ions on the surface of clay platelets, which reduces the electrical double layer and induces clay flocculation; followed by long-term pozzolanic reactions wherein free calcium hydroxide ($	ext{Ca(OH)}_2$) reacts with reactive silica and alumina dissolved from kaolinitic clays to form secondary calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium-aluminate-hydrate (C-A-H) gels. These mineral gels permanently occlude capillary soil pores, reducing total water absorption to 7.0–11.5% and imparting high spray erosion resistance (Danso et al., 2015; Audu et al., 2025; Sesay et al., 2025).

Crucially, when benchmarked against commercial sandcrete hollow masonry blocks sold across informal West and East African markets, CSEBs demonstrate decisive structural superiority. While artisanal sandcrete blocks routinely collapse under compressive stresses of 1.0 to 1.8 MPa due to extreme cement dilution and inadequate curing, certified CSEBs achieve 3.5 to 9.2 MPa, comfortably exceeding Nigerian Industrial Standard NIS 87 (minimum 2.5 MPa) and Ghanaian GS 297 (minimum 2.8 MPa) requirements. Furthermore, modern stabilized rammed earth walls (compacted pneumatically in continuous formwork with 6–8% cement) achieve structural compressive capacities of 4.5 to 8.5 MPa, delivering thermal mass with thermal conductivity values between 0.65 and 0.95 W/m·K (compared to 1.30–1.75 W/m·K for dense concrete), substantially improving indoor thermal comfort in Sahelian and tropical savannah climatic zones.

## 3.4 Deep-Dive Synthesis: Low-Carbon Binders & Supplementary Cementitious Materials
The 995 studies investigating low-carbon binders represent the technical vanguard of African industrial decarbonization. Research primarily branches into two complementary paradigms: Limestone Calcined Clay Cement (LC3) and agricultural by-product pozzolanic ashes.

### Limestone Calcined Clay Cement (LC3)
Pioneered globally by Scrivener et al. (2018, 2019), LC3 technology achieves clinker factor reductions of up to 50% by substituting clinker with a blend of calcined kaolinitic clay (typically 30%), uncalcined limestone powder (15%), and gypsum (5%). Empirical trials across African jurisdictions—including Burkina Faso, Egypt, South Africa, and Nigeria—confirm that low-purity kaolinitic clays containing as little as 40% to 50% kaolinite, which are unsuitable for ceramic or paper manufacturing, achieve optimal reactivity when calcined at moderate temperatures between 700°C and 800°C (Nshimiyimana et al., 2025; Shamseldein et al., 2025).

The synergistic hydration of LC3 involves the reaction of metakaolin with calcium hydroxide ($	ext{Ca(OH)}_2$) released during clinker hydration, forming stratlingite and C-S-H. Crucially, the presence of limestone provides calcium carbonate ($	ext{CaCO}_3$), which reacts with alumina phases to precipitate mono-carboaluminate and hemi-carboaluminate, preventing the conversion of ettringite to monosulfate. This chemical mechanism dramatically refines the capillary pore structure of the hardened paste, resulting in concrete compressive strengths of 28.0 to 52.0 MPa at 28 days (Table 3), matching or exceeding 100% Ordinary Portland Cement concrete (BS EN 206 C25/30 to C40/50). Furthermore, the refined pore network provides outstanding durability, reducing rapid chloride permeability by 60% to 80% and mitigating sulfate attack, making LC3 extraordinarily well suited for aggressive coastal marine environments across African ports and coastal cities.

### Agricultural Waste Pozzolans
Sub-Saharan Africa generates tens of millions of tonnes of agricultural processing residues annually, the vast majority of which are burned in open fields or dumped into landfills. Empirical investigations across Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and Côte d'Ivoire demonstrate that controlled thermal processing of agricultural wastes yields highly reactive supplementary cementitious materials:
1. Rice Husk Ash (RHA): When incinerated under controlled conditions between 600°C and 700°C for 2 hours, RHA produces an amorphous silica ($	ext{SiO}_2$) content exceeding 85% to 92% with a high specific surface area. Substituting 10% to 20% of OPC with RHA produces concretes achieving 28-day compressive strengths of 26.0 to 44.0 MPa, while accelerating early hydration and densifying the interfacial transition zone.
2. Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA): Generated by cogeneration boilers in sugar processing mills in Southern and East Africa (Kenya, South Africa, Mauritius, Zimbabwe), SCBA contains 65% to 78% silica. At 10% to 15% replacement, SCBA concrete achieves structural strengths comparable to standard C25 concrete, while reducing drying shrinkage (Thomas et al., 2018; Alaneme et al., 2025).
3. Palm Oil Fuel Ash (POFA) and Cassava Peel Ash (CPA): Extensively investigated across West Africa's agricultural belts, POFA and CPA achieve effective pozzolanic activity indices (>75% per ASTM C618) at 10% to 15% replacement levels, offering viable localized pathways to reduce concrete cost and embodied carbon (Ogunro et al., 2025; Aprianti et al., 2015).

### Alkali-Activated Geopolymer Binders
Alkali-activated materials and geopolymers synthesized from industrial blast furnace slag, coal fly ash (extensively available in South Africa), and metakaolin demonstrate exceptional compressive strengths (30.0–65.0 MPa) and rapid strength development without requiring Portland clinker. While geopolymers eliminate clinker-associated emissions entirely, their continental adoption remains constrained by the cost, corrosive handling hazards, and localized availability of concentrated chemical activators (sodium hydroxide and sodium silicate solutions), directing primary African application toward precast manufacturing facilities rather than in-situ site casting (Davidovits, 2020; Provis & van Deventer, 2014; Ikotun et al., 2025).

## 3.5 Deep-Dive Synthesis: Recycled Aggregates & Industrial Solid Waste Composites
With accelerating urban renewal and infrastructure expansion, African metropolises are generating millions of tonnes of construction and demolition waste (CDW) alongside staggering volumes of municipal solid waste, which are routinely dumped in open landfills or waterways. Across 444 included studies, researchers have characterized the structural viability of integrating recycled materials into concrete and masonry.

### Recycled Concrete Aggregate (RCA)
Empirical trials across South Africa, Nigeria, and Egypt establish that crushed concrete demolition rubble can successfully replace virgin granite or basalt coarse aggregates. At replacement levels between 20% and 40%, RCA concretes achieve compressive strengths of 20.0 to 36.0 MPa, fully satisfying structural grade C20/25 requirements. However, coarse RCA particles retain porous residual adhered cement mortar, which increases aggregate water absorption (4.0–8.5% for RCA vs. 0.5–1.5% for natural gravel) and weakens the interfacial transition zone (ITZ). To mitigate this workability loss and strength reduction, empirical studies validate the deployment of multi-stage surface treatment protocols—such as pre-soaking RCA in pozzolanic silica fume slurries or diatomaceous earth suspensions (Dicha et al., 2025; Prasittisopin & Trejo, 2025)—which densify the porous adhered mortar and restore mechanical performance to near-virgin concrete baselines.

### Post-Consumer Plastic Wastes in Masonry
The accumulation of post-consumer polyethylene terephthalate (PET) beverage bottles, high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bags, and polypropylene containers represents a major environmental hazard in African cities. Empirical research has demonstrated two primary structural recycling pathways:
1. Sand-Plastic Composite Pavers: Shredded post-consumer plastics are thermally melted at 180°C–220°C and blended with river sand or quarry dust at ratios of 1:2 to 1:4 (plastic to sand) without any cement or water. Upon hydraulic compression, these composite paving blocks achieve compressive strengths of 14.0 to 28.0 MPa, with water absorption rates below 1.5%, exhibiting extraordinary abrasion resistance and superior flexural toughness compared to standard concrete pavers (Babafemi et al., 2021; Agyeman et al., 2019).
2. Shredded Plastic Concrete Aggregates: Substituting 5% to 15% of fine aggregate with shredded plastic flakes reduces concrete density by 8% to 15% and increases impact resistance and ductility, although replacement levels beyond 15% cause significant reductions in compressive strength due to hydrophobic debonding at the polymer-cement paste interface (Abhi et al., 2025).

### Industrial By-Products and Agricultural Shells
Additional circular material solutions include Crushed Palm Kernel Shells (PKS), which have been successfully developed across Nigeria and Ghana as sustainable coarse aggregates for structural lightweight concrete, achieving densities of 1,750 to 1,950 kg/m3 and compressive capacities of 17.0 to 24.0 MPa (Ndububa & Ndububa, 2018); and Crushed Container Glass, which, when ground to micro-particulate size (<75 µm), suppresses the alkali-silica reaction (ASR) and acts as an effective secondary pozzolan (Olofinnade et al., 2018).

## 3.6 Deep-Dive Synthesis: Renewable Bio-Based Structural Materials
The 358 included studies on bio-based systems highlight Africa's immense, yet severely underutilized, forestry and non-timber forest product reserves. Structural bamboo represents the foremost candidate for continental structural bio-decarbonization.

### Structural Bamboo: The "Green Steel" of Tropical Africa
Indigenous bamboo species—most notably *Oxytenanthera abyssinica* (widely distributed across the dry savannah and woodlands of East, West, and Central Africa), *Bambusa vulgaris* (abundant in humid coastal rainforest belts), and *Yushania alpina* (highland bamboo in the mountain ecosystems of Ethiopia, Kenya, and Rwanda)—exhibit extraordinary mechanical strength-to-weight ratios (INBAR, 2020; Kibwage et al., 2017). Empirical axial tensile testing of clear bamboo culms consistently demonstrates ultimate tensile capacities between 120 and 240 MPa (Table 3), surpassing standard structural mild steel (yield strength ~250 MPa) on a strength-to-density basis, with compressive capacities along the fiber axis reaching 45 to 78 MPa (Trujillo et al., 2016; van der Lugt et al., 2006).

Despite these remarkable structural properties, historical deployment of bamboo in African permanent construction has been crippled by rapid biological degradation. Untreated bamboo culms contain high concentrations of starch, hemicellulose, and free sugars, rendering them acutely susceptible to attack by powder-post beetles (*Dinoderus minutus*), subterranean termites, and rot fungi within 6 to 24 months of outdoor exposure. Decadal empirical research has resolved this vulnerability through the standardization of low-toxicity chemical diffusion preservation: soaking green bamboo culms in an aqueous solution of 5% borax (disodium octaborate tetrahydrate) and boric acid (1:1 ratio) for 7 to 14 days, or utilizing vertical sap displacement (Boucherie process), leaches starch and renders the culms permanently toxic to insect borers and fungal spores, extending structural service life to over 40 years under covered conditions (Salzer et al., 2016; Zea Escamilla & Habert, 2014).

### Plant Fiber Reinforcement in Earth and Mortar
The incorporation of vegetable fibers—including sisal (*Agave sisalana*), coconut coir, banana pseudostem, and oil palm mesocarp fibers—at 0.5% to 2.0% volume fraction into earth blocks and cementitious renders substantially enhances post-cracking ductility, elevates flexural toughness by 40% to 110%, and arrests plastic shrinkage cracking during rapid tropical drying regimes (Danso, 2018; Akintade et al., 2025; Kilani et al., 2025).

## 3.7 Deep-Dive Synthesis: Innovative & Smart Passive Cooling Systems
Representing 5.0% of included studies (n = 141), innovative material systems address the escalating demand for operational building energy efficiency under severe climate change. With tropical cooling loads projected to account for a massive surge in continental electricity demand, passive thermal regulation materials provide critical non-mechanical cooling solutions.

Phase Change Materials (PCMs)—specifically organic paraffin waxes and bio-based fatty acids derived from coconut and palm oils—possess high latent heat of fusion (140–210 J/g) at phase transition temperatures engineered between 24°C and 28°C. Micro-encapsulated PCMs blended into interior gypsum plasters, unfired earth walling, or cement ceiling panels absorb excess solar thermal gains during peak daytime hours through isothermal melting, and release heat during nighttime ventilation through crystallization (Cabeza et al., 2011). Empirical prototype testing in North and West African climates confirms that PCM-integrated building envelopes attenuate indoor diurnal temperature fluctuations by 3.5°C to 7.0°C, drastically mitigating mechanical air conditioning energy requirements.

Concurrently, microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP) utilizing ureolytic bacteria (*Sporosarcina pasteurii*) has been validated for bio-mediated soil stabilization and concrete crack self-healing. By hydrolyzing urea in the presence of calcium chloride, bacterial cells serve as nucleation sites for calcium carbonate ($	ext{CaCO}_3$) precipitation, sealing microcracks up to 0.4 mm in width and restoring concrete compressive strength by 15% to 25% (De Belie et al., 2018).

## 4.2 Structural Reliability versus Market Realities: The Sandcrete Paradox
A central finding of this decadal systematic review is what we term the "Sandcrete Paradox": while laboratory and field evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates that Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEBs yielding 3.5–9.2 MPa) and low-carbon binders (LC3 yielding 28–52 MPa) are structurally superior, environmentally benign, and cost-competitive, commercial construction across African cities remains overwhelmingly dominated by structurally deficient artisanal sandcrete blocks yielding a meager 1.0 to 1.8 MPa (Table 3).

This persistence is driven by powerful socio-economic, perceptual, and regulatory distortions:
1. The Perverse Incentive of Artisanal Dilution: Sandcrete block manufacturing is an informal, low-capital enterprise. In the absence of regulatory enforcement, artisanal producers dilute Portland cement to extreme ratios (e.g., one 50-kg bag of cement producing 45 to 55 nine-inch hollow blocks, rather than the regulatory standard of 25 to 30 blocks). Because end-users and informal private developers lack portable compressive testing gauges at construction sites, substandard blocks with compressive strengths as low as 1.0 MPa are routinely purchased based solely on visual appearance and lowest purchase price (Adeyemi & Idoro, 2012; Dahiru et al., 2014; Ede et al., 2014).
2. The "Poverty Stigma" of Earth Architecture: Decades of colonial-era building policies created an enduring social stigma that associates earth-based and indigenous materials with poverty, rural backwardness, and temporary shelter. Modern urban clients, aspiring to social mobility, aggressively demand concrete and cement plaster—perceiving cement as the exclusive symbol of permanence, safety, and modern wealth—even when the underlying sandcrete blocks are structurally fragile and prone to moisture decay (Ala et al., 2025; Swati Sinha et al., 2025; Puttamanjaiah et al., 2025).
3. The Colonial Legacy of Prescriptive Codes: Building regulations across most Anglophone and Francophone African nations remain archaic transcriptions of mid-20th-century European building codes. These codes are prescriptive rather than performance-based, explicitly requiring "Portland cement concrete" and "sandcrete masonry" for structural approval, while lacking approved testing pathways for CSEBs, bamboo space frames, or geopolymer binders. Structural engineers specifying alternative materials risk having municipal planning approvals rejected or incurring professional liability.

## 4.3 Empirical Barriers-versus-Enablers Matrix: Systemic Decarbonization Roadmap
Synthesizing adoption data across the 2,822 studies and complementary industrial surveys (Agboola et al., 2024; Windapo & Goulding, 2015; Mangoro et al., 2025; Benham et al., 2025; Mahame et al., 2025) delineates four interconnected systemic barrier categories alongside empirical enablers:

### 1. Regulatory & Institutional Dimension (78% Barrier Prevalence)
- Barriers: Archaic, prescriptive colonial-era building regulations that lack codification for non-Portland binders and earth masonry; complete absence of green building procurement mandates in federal civil projects.
- Validated Enablers: Expedited national gazetting and legal integration of the African Organisation for Standardisation (ARSO) regional standards (ARS 680–684 for Compressed Earth Blocks); transition to performance-based national building codes (evaluating load capacity, fire rating, and moisture durability rather than raw material recipe); introduction of mandatory 25% minimum green material quotas in all public housing, schools, and civic infrastructure contracts.

### 2. Supply Chain & Economic Dimension (72% Barrier Prevalence)
- Barriers: Extreme fragmentation in agricultural biomass and waste collection (rice husk and bagasse ash are dispersed across millions of smallholder farms); lack of commercial-scale calcining kilns for LC3; high upfront capital cost for hydraulic automated earth block presses.
- Validated Enablers: Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) establishing regional centralized calcination hubs for kaolinitic clays adjacent to agricultural processing clusters; formalization of agricultural cooperative collection networks to supply agro-waste ashes to centralized cement blending plants; zero-rating import tariffs on commercial-grade hydraulic block compaction machinery and testing equipment.

### 3. Technical & Infrastructure Dimension (64% Barrier Prevalence)
- Barriers: Severe deficits in local laboratory materials characterization facilities (XRF, XRD, calibrated UTMs) outside premier capital universities; shortage of formally trained site artisans skilled in moisture-density optimization, soil grading, and chemical stabilization.
- Validated Enablers: Establishment of accredited regional materials testing centers and mobile calibration units; full integration of compressed earth masonry, bamboo jointing, and bio-composite fabrication into national Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) curricula and national trade apprenticeships.

### 4. Socio-Cultural & Perceptual Dimension (61% Barrier Prevalence)
- Barriers: Enduring cultural perception equating earth and bio-based materials with rural poverty; commercial skepticism regarding long-term tropical weathering durability.
- Validated Enablers: Construction of prominent, prestigious civic showcase architecture (presidential libraries, university faculty buildings, ministry headquarters, high-end ecotourism lodges) deploying exposed stabilized earth, structural bamboo, and LC3 concrete; public education campaigns highlighting superior indoor thermal comfort, fire safety, and acoustic performance.

## 4.4 Methodological Gaps & Review Limitations
While this systematic review provides an unprecedented empirical baseline, two major methodological gaps within the current literature must be explicitly acknowledged:
1. Persistent Scarcity of Longitudinal Durability Data: While 100% of included studies report standard 28-day laboratory compressive capacities, fewer than 15% provide multi-year outdoor weathering evaluations, cyclic wetting-and-drying durability (e.g., ASTM D559), or seismic cyclic loading data under tropical monsoon conditions. The long-term performance of bio-stabilizers, cassava starch binders, and plastic aggregates beyond five years remains severely under-documented.
2. Over-Reliance on Foreign LCA Background Databases: The vast majority of published Life Cycle Assessments (LCAs) in the African literature rely on generic European background databases (e.g., Ecoinvent) rather than localized African grid emission factors, regional heavy fuel oil logistics, and artisanal production dynamics. Developing localized, publicly accessible African Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) databases is an urgent research priority for continental environmental engineering.

# 5. Conclusions & Strategic Action Roadmap (2026–2030)

## 5.1 Principal Findings
Synthesizing a decadal evidence base of 2,822 peer-reviewed empirical studies published between 2015 and 2025 across sovereign African nations yields definitive conclusions regarding the structural, ecological, and economic viability of sustainable building materials:
1. Structural Decarbonization is Empirically Validated: African sustainable materials possess physical and mechanical capacities that fully match or exceed conventional carbon-intensive baselines. Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEBs yielding 3.5–9.2 MPa) provide 2 to 3 times the compressive capacity of the substandard artisanal sandcrete blocks (1.0–1.8 MPa) that currently dominate informal markets, while utilizing 50% to 70% less cement.
2. Low-Carbon Binders Match Structural Concrete: Limestone Calcined Clay Cement (LC3) and agricultural pozzolanic concretes (rice husk ash, sugarcane bagasse ash, palm oil fuel ash) achieve full structural grades (25.0–52.0 MPa), delivering an 18% to 42% reduction in embodied carbon and superior resistance to marine chloride attack.
3. Bio-Based Materials Offer Active Carbon Sequestration: Indigenous structural bamboo (*Oxytenanthera abyssinica* and *Bambusa vulgaris*) provides ultimate tensile capacities of 120 to 240 MPa and net negative carbon storage (-120 to -350 kg CO2-eq/m3), providing a nature-based pathway to transform building superstructures into terrestrial carbon sinks.
4. Adoption is Constrained by Policy and Perception, Not Engineering Viability: The primary obstacles preventing continental scale are not technical or structural deficits, but institutional inertia, archaic prescriptive building regulations, fragmented supply chains, and outdated social stigmas.

## 5.2 Strategic Action Roadmap (2026–2030)
To transition sustainable building materials from academic laboratory benches to mainstream continental infrastructure deployment, the following five priority strategic actions are urgently recommended:
1. Regulatory & Standards Harmonization: National standardization bureaus (e.g., SON in Nigeria, GSA in Ghana, SABS in South Africa, KEBS in Kenya) must formally gazette and integrate ARSO ARS 680–684 regional earth block codes into national building acts by 2027, transitioning national building approval mechanisms from prescriptive recipe-based mandates to performance-based criteria.
2. Green Public Procurement Quotas: African national governments and regional development finance institutions (AfDB, Africa50, Shelter Afrique) must enforce mandatory minimum quotas requiring at least 25% of all walling and structural materials in publicly funded social housing, educational, and healthcare infrastructure to comprise certified sustainable materials (CSEBs, LC3, or bamboo).
3. Decentralized Industrial Calcination Hubs: Establish national public-private partnerships (PPPs) to construct regional gas- or biomass-fired rotary kilns for calcining low-purity kaolinitic clays (for LC3) and processing agricultural biomass ash, overcoming supply chain fragmentation.
4. Vocational TVET Artisan Certification: Fully incorporate compressed earth masonry production, hydraulic press operation, and bamboo preservation/jointing into national Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) curricula, creating a certified cadre of green construction artisans.
5. Prestige Civic Showcase Projects: National ministries of infrastructure must commission prominent public civic structures utilizing exposed stabilized earth, structural bamboo, and LC3 concrete, dispelling the stigma of alternative materials as "poor man's housing" and demonstrating architectural excellence.
