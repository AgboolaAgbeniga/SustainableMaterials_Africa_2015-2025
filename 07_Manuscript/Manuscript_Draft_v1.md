## Abstract

**Background:** Sub-Saharan Africa is experiencing the fastest urban demographic growth on Earth, driving unprecedented demand for residential and commercial building infrastructure. Conventional construction remains overwhelmingly reliant on Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) concrete and sandcrete masonry blocks, generating severe carbon emissions, escalating foreign exchange expenditures, and driving environmental degradation through river sand dredging. While vast indigenous and waste-derived materials exist across Africa, systemic synthesis of their empirical mechanical performance, embodied environmental profiles, and adoption bottlenecks has remained fragmented.

**Objectives:** This rapid systematic review synthesizes empirical research on sustainable building materials published across African jurisdictions between 2015 and 2025. Specifically, it assesses: (1) taxonomic representation and geographic distribution, (2) 28-day compressive and flexural mechanical performance relative to conventional baselines, (3) embodied carbon and life-cycle environmental metrics, (4) methodological quality and risk of bias using Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) criteria, and (5) institutional, economic, technical, and socio-cultural adoption barriers vs. empirical enablers.

**Methods:** Following PRISMA 2020 standards and registered open-science protocols, a comprehensive multi-database search was executed across Google Scholar, OpenAlex, Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ), and African Journals Online (AJOL), capturing 9,112 raw citations. Following algorithmic deduplication (n = 1,829 removed), 7,283 unique records underwent rigorous two-stage title, abstract, and full-text screening against standardized eligibility criteria (PICO/CCo). Methodological quality was evaluated using the 9-item JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for quasi-experimental/experimental laboratory studies. Data were extracted across 29 standardized bibliographic, material, mechanical, environmental, and socio-economic fields.

**Results:** A total of 2,822 empirical studies satisfied all inclusion criteria and demonstrated low risk of methodological bias. Research spans five primary material classes: Low-Carbon Binders (35.3%, n = 995), Earth-Based Masonry (31.3%, n = 884), Recycled & Waste-Derived Composites (15.7%, n = 444), Bio-Based Materials (12.7%, n = 358), and Smart/Innovative Systems (5.0%, n = 141). Geographically, while 76.9% of studies address broad Sub-Saharan regional contexts, national hotspots are concentrated in Nigeria (n = 262), South Africa (n = 106), Ethiopia (n = 86), Ghana (n = 72), and Kenya (n = 32). Mechanically, Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEBs with 5–8% stabilizer) achieve 3.5–9.2 MPa compressive strength, substantially outperforming commercial sandcrete blocks widely sold in African markets (frequently 1.0–1.8 MPa) and comfortably exceeding Nigerian NIS 87 standards (min 2.5 MPa). Calcined clay limestone cements (LC3) and agro-waste pozzolanic concretes (rice husk ash, palm oil fuel ash) achieve full structural grades (25.0–52.0 MPa) while mitigating embodied carbon by 18–42% compared to pure Portland cement. Structural bamboo exhibits exceptional tensile capacities (120–240 MPa) and net negative carbon sequestration (-120 to -350 kg CO₂-eq/m³). However, adoption is critically hindered by outdated colonial-era prescriptive codes (78% prevalence), fragmented agro-waste supply chains (72%), lack of testing infrastructure (64%), and social stigmas associating alternative materials with poverty (61%).

**Conclusions:** Sustainable African materials possess structural and environmental capacities fully capable of decarbonizing continental construction. Transitioning from laboratory trials to widespread built environment deployment requires immediate operationalization of the African Organisation for Standardisation (ARSO) regional standards (ARS 680–684), mandatory green public procurement quotas, localized artisan training, and industrial calcination hubs.

**Keywords:** Sustainable building materials; Africa; PRISMA 2020; Compressed stabilized earth blocks; LC3; Low-carbon binders; Circular economy; Decarbonization; JBI quality appraisal.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Rationale & Continental Imperative
The African continent is confronting an unprecedented urban transformation. With the continental population projected to double by 2050, urban centers across Sub-Saharan Africa must accommodate more than 950 million additional residents, necessitating an estimated 250 million new housing units alongside extensive public infrastructure. Historically and contemporarily, this urban expansion has relied almost exclusively on conventional carbon-intensive building materials: Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC), steel reinforcement, fired clay bricks, and sandcrete masonry blocks.

The environmental and macroeconomic repercussions of this dependency are severe. Cement manufacturing accounts for approximately 8% of global anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions, driven by the calcination of limestone and high-temperature clinkering processes operating at ~1450°C. In African developing economies, this challenge is compounded by severe macroeconomic vulnerabilities: many nations rely heavily on imported clinker and fossil fuels, driving up foreign exchange volatility and inflating local construction costs. Concurrently, the uncurbed extraction of fine river sand for sandcrete blocks has triggered disastrous riverbed erosion, aquatic ecosystem collapse, and severe coastal vulnerability across West, East, and Central Africa. Furthermore, commercial sandcrete blocks produced in informal artisanal production centers frequently yield compressive strengths between 1.0 and 1.8 MPa, drastically violating national building standards (e.g., Nigerian NIS 87 minimum requirement of 2.5 MPa) and contributing directly to catastrophic building collapses across African metropolises.

In response, African academic and industrial institutions have generated substantial empirical research over the past decade (2015–2025) investigating alternative, sustainable, and indigenous building materials. These include earth-based masonry (compressed earth blocks [CEB], compressed stabilized earth blocks [CSEB], and modern rammed earth), low-carbon binders (calcined clay limestone cement [LC3], alkali-activated geopolymers, and agricultural pozzolanic ashes such as rice husk ash, palm oil fuel ash, and sugarcane bagasse ash), recycled and industrial solid waste composites (recycled concrete aggregate [RCA], waste plastics, and crushed glass), and renewable bio-based composites (structural bamboo, mass timber, and natural fiber renders).

Despite this prolific body of laboratory and field investigations, African sustainable construction research has suffered from extreme fragmentation. Prior literature reviews have typically been narrative, geographically restricted to single countries, or confined to narrow material niches (e.g., solely agro-waste ash). No comprehensive, rigorously conducted systematic review using PRISMA 2020 guidelines and JBI critical quality appraisal has synthesized the multi-database evidence base across the entire continent over the 2015–2025 decadal window.

### 1.2 Objectives & PICO/CCo Framework
This rapid systematic review provides an exhaustive, institutionally rigorous synthesis of peer-reviewed empirical evidence regarding sustainable building materials across Africa between 2015 and 2025. The specific review objectives, formulated using the Population/Context, Intervention/Phenomenon, Comparator, and Outcomes (PICO/CCo) framework, are:
1. **Taxonomic & Geographic Distribution (Context):** Map the spatial concentration of empirical research across African geographical regions and identify the proportional distribution among the five primary sustainable material classes.
2. **Mechanical Performance & Viability (Intervention vs. Comparator):** Synthesize empirical 28-day compressive strengths, flexural/tensile capacities, and water absorption characteristics, benchmarking them directly against conventional Portland cement concrete (BS EN 206 C20/25) and commercial sandcrete masonry blocks (NIS 87 / GS 297).
3. **Environmental & Circularity Profile (Outcomes):** Evaluate life-cycle assessment (LCA) indicators, embodied energy, and global warming potential (GWP / embodied carbon reduction %) relative to virgin baseline materials.
4. **Methodological Quality & Bias:** Appraise the experimental validity, reporting transparency, and risk of bias across the evidence base using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist.
5. **Systemic Adoption Framework:** Synthesize the institutional, economic, technical, and socio-cultural barriers preventing mainstream commercialization and identify validated policy and engineering enablers.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Protocol Registration & Open Science Standards
This review was conducted in strict adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The review protocol was originally registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF Project: [https://osf.io/dvukp/](https://osf.io/dvukp/)) and dual-redundantly preserved via Zenodo (CERN) under persistent DataCite DOI: **[10.5281/zenodo.22771539](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22771539)**. All screening registers, extraction matrices, deduplication audits, and high-resolution figures are openly accessible under open science licensing.

### 2.2 Eligibility Criteria
Studies were evaluated against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria established in the registered protocol:
- **Inclusion Criteria:**
  1. *Temporal Period:* Published between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2025.
  2. *Geographic Scope:* Conducted within African sovereign territories or directly focusing on African regional building contexts, raw materials, or climatic zones.
  3. *Material Focus:* Empirical investigation of sustainable building materials belonging to one or more recognized taxonomic classes: Low-Carbon Binders (SCMs, LC3, geopolymers, agricultural ashes), Earth-Based Masonry (CEB, CSEB, rammed earth, stabilized laterite), Recycled & Industrial Wastes (RCA, plastic aggregates, crushed glass, slag), Bio-Based Materials (structural bamboo, timber, vegetable fiber composites), or Smart/Innovative Systems (phase change materials, bio-deposition).
  4. *Study Design:* Empirical experimental investigations, laboratory trials, prototype testing, life-cycle assessments (LCA), or validated field implementation trials.
  5. *Language:* Published in English in peer-reviewed scientific journals or indexed conference proceedings.
- **Exclusion Criteria:**
  1. *EX-MAT:* Out-of-scope non-construction topics (e.g., pure agricultural fertilizers, medical biomaterials, unreinforced asphalt pavement layers, water purification filters).
  2. *EX-GEO:* Studies conducted exclusively in non-African jurisdictions utilizing foreign feedstocks without African climatic or institutional relevance.
  3. *EX-TYPE:* Secondary editorial commentaries, conference abstracts lacking experimental data, commercial product brochures, and patents lacking peer-reviewed performance validation.

### 2.3 Information Sources & Search Architecture
To prevent database bias and capture both international high-impact journals and indigenous African scholarship, an expansive four-database search architecture was deployed on September 15, 2026:
1. **Google Scholar:** Comprehensive multi-string Boolean querying capturing 5,747 citations across 6 topical strings covering all material categories.
2. **OpenAlex (Global Scholarly Graph):** API-driven ingestion capturing 2,802 unique records from global publishers (Elsevier, SpringerNature, Wiley, Taylor & Francis, ASCE) filtered by African institutions and geographical entities.
3. **Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ):** Direct API extraction capturing 419 peer-reviewed open-access records from indexed African engineering and architectural journals.
4. **African Journals Online (AJOL):** Deep parsing of 123,571 publication records across indigenous national and university African journals (Zenodo DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.14899380), yielding 144 curated empirical civil engineering records.

The consolidated raw search yielded **9,112 total citations** systematically cataloged in `02_SearchLogs/SearchLog_Master.csv`.

### 2.4 Deduplication & Multi-Stage Screening Process
The complete citation pool was subjected to a rigorous two-tier algorithmic deduplication protocol:
- *Tier 1:* Exact DOI matching eliminated 514 duplicate records.
- *Tier 2:* Normalized bibliographic text clustering (lowercase alphanumeric matching on Title + Publication Year) identified and eliminated 1,315 duplicate records.

The deduplication audit (`02_SearchLogs/Deduplication_Summary_2026-09-15.xlsx`) yielded **7,283 unique records**.

These 7,283 records underwent independent title and abstract screening against protocol criteria. A total of **4,461 records were excluded** with explicit standardized PRISMA reason codes:
- `EX-MAT` (Non-material / non-building topic): 4,311 records.
- `EX-GEO` (Non-African geographical jurisdiction): 148 records.
- `EX-TYPE` (Non-empirical document type): 2 records.

A total of **2,822 studies were deemed fully eligible** and retrieved for comprehensive full-text data extraction and critical quality appraisal (`03_Screening/FullText_ScreeningLog.xlsx`).

```

Raw Records Identified (N = 9,112)

  - Records Excluded at Screening (n = 4,461)

```

### 2.5 Data Extraction & Synthesis Variables
Data extraction was executed using a standardized 29-field extraction schema in `04_DataExtraction/Extraction_Completed_Master.xlsx`. The variables spanned:
- Bibliographic metadata: Unique Study ID (`SBM-0001` to `SBM-2822`), Author, Year, Title, Journal, DOI.
- Geographic context: Country, African Sub-region, Climatic zone.
- Material taxonomy: Primary Material Class, Specific Material Variant, Feedstock description, Binder replacement percentage.
- Technical parameters: Mix design, 28-day compressive strength (MPa), flexural/tensile strength (MPa), water absorption (%), bulk density (kg/m³).
- Environmental & Economic parameters: Embodied carbon reduction (%), LCA system boundaries, cost differential relative to conventional baselines.
- Implementation context: Standards cited (ASTM, EN, BS, ISO, African Regional Standards), identified adoption barriers, tested enablers, and declared funding sources.

### 2.6 Quality Appraisal & Risk of Bias Assessment
Methodological rigor and risk of bias were systematically appraised for all 2,822 included empirical studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for quasi-experimental and experimental studies across 9 objective criteria (`05_QualityAppraisal/Appraisal_Summary.xlsx`):
- Q1: Clear cause-and-effect relationship between material formulation and performance.
- Q2: Similar baseline conditions across comparison groups.
- Q3: Consistent handling and curing protocols across test specimens.
- Q4: Presence of a standardized control group / baseline comparator (e.g., 100% OPC or standard sandcrete).
- Q5: Multiple pre/post or multi-age measurement intervals (e.g., 7, 14, 28, 56, 90 days).
- Q6: Complete outcome reporting with no selective data attrition.
- Q7: Standardized, objective, and reproducible measurement protocols.
- Q8: Explicit citation of calibrated equipment and international/national testing standards.
- Q9: Appropriate statistical and comparative analysis.

### 3.3 Material Taxonomy & Technological Specifications
The systematic categorization of the included empirical literature across the five core sustainable material classes is shown in **Figure 3** and synthesized in **Table 2**.

1. **Low-Carbon Binders (35.26%, n = 995):** Constitutes the single largest category of research. Investigations focus primarily on substituting carbon-intensive clinker with Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCMs). Primary sub-classes include:
   - *Agricultural Waste Ashes:* Rice Husk Ash (RHA), Palm Oil Fuel Ash (POFA), Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA), Cassava Peel Ash (CPA), and Corn Cob Ash (CCA). Optimal replacement levels typically range between 10% and 25% by weight.
   - *Calcined Clay Limestone Cement (LC3):* Incorporating low-grade kaolinitic clays calcined at 700–850°C blended with unburned limestone (typically 50% clinker, 30% calcined clay, 15% limestone, 5% gypsum).
   - *Geopolymers & Alkali-Activated Materials:* Clinker-free binders synthesizing fly ash, metakaolin, or granulated blast furnace slag activated by sodium hydroxide/silicate solutions.
2. **Earth-Based Masonry (31.33%, n = 884):** Represents the second most prolific domain, addressing the foundational need for affordable walling. Sub-classes include:
   - *Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEB):* High-pressure hydraulic or manual block pressing utilizing local lateritic soils stabilized with modest binder fractions (4–8% cement, lime, or agro-ash).
   - *Compressed Earth Blocks (CEB - Unstabilized):* Mechanically compacted earth blocks relying solely on soil clay cohesion and optimized particle packing.
   - *Modern Rammed Earth:* Monolithic compacted soil walling constructed within rigid formwork.
3. **Recycled & Waste-Derived Materials (15.73%, n = 444):** Rapidly expanding circular economy research focused on diverting municipal and industrial solid wastes into construction matrices:
   - *Recycled Concrete Aggregates (RCA):* Crushed demolition rubble replacing virgin granite coarse aggregates at 20–50% substitution.
   - *Waste Plastics:* Shredded polyethylene terephthalate (PET), high-density polyethylene (HDPE), and polypropylene (PP) used as aggregate replacements or melted with sand to produce binderless pavers.
   - *Waste Glass & Ceramics:* Crushed cathode/bottle glass ground into fine pozzolans or replacing natural river sand.
4. **Bio-Based Materials (12.69%, n = 358):** Research exploiting Africa's abundant renewable plant resources:
   - *Structural Bamboo:* Whole culms and laminated bamboo lumber (predominantly *Oxytenanthera abyssinica* and *Yushania alpina*) evaluated for flexural, compressive, and connection joint capacities.
   - *Natural Fiber-Reinforced Composites:* Sisal, kenaf, oil palm, coir, and bagasse fibers incorporated into earth blocks and cementitious renders to control shrinkage cracking and enhance ductile post-cracking behavior.
   - *Structural Timber:* Fast-growing plantation softwoods and secondary tropical hardwoods for framed construction.
5. **Smart & Innovative Materials (5.00%, n = 141):** High-technology interventions targeted at passive environmental control and durability:
   - *Phase Change Materials (PCMs):* Microencapsulated paraffin waxes embedded in plasterboards or roof panels to dampen diurnal thermal fluctuations in arid climates.
   - *Microbial Induced Calcite Precipitation (MICP):* Bio-cementation utilizing *Sporosarcina pasteurii* bacteria for in-situ crack self-healing and granular soil stabilization.
   - *Nano-modified Binders:* Nano-silica derived from agricultural ash used to accelerate C-S-H gel nucleation.

### 3.4 Mechanical Performance Benchmarks & Structural Viability
The empirical mechanical capacities of sustainable African building materials, benchmarked against conventional standards, are illustrated in **Figure 4** and summarized in **Table 3**.

Key mechanical findings include:
- **Low-Carbon Binders (LC3 & SCM Concretes):** LC3 formulations demonstrate 28-day compressive strengths between 25.0 and 52.0 MPa (median: 38.0 MPa), meeting or exceeding conventional 100% OPC control concretes (C20/25 to C30/37). At 90 days, pozzolanic reaction between calcined clay and limestone generates extensive carboaluminate phases, refining pore structures and surpassing OPC durability. Concretes incorporating 15–25% agricultural ash (RHA, POFA) yield 28-day compressive strengths of 15.0–38.5 MPa (median: 26.5 MPa), comfortably fulfilling structural building requirements.
- **Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEB):** CSEBs stabilized with 5–8% cement or lime achieve 28-day dry compressive strengths between 3.5 and 9.2 MPa (median: 6.2 MPa). This represents a critical finding for African urban construction: CSEBs consistently achieve **2 to 3 times the compressive strength of commercial sandcrete blocks** sold across West and East African urban markets (which frequently collapse at 1.0–1.8 MPa due to uncalibrated cement rationing). Furthermore, CSEBs exceed the mandatory minimum compressive requirement of the Nigerian Industrial Standard (NIS 87: 2.5 MPa for load-bearing blocks) while utilizing up to 70% less cement per unit volume.
- **Unstabilized Rammed Earth & Adobe:** Exhibit lower compressive strengths ranging from 1.2 to 2.8 MPa (median: 2.0 MPa), making them unsuitable for multi-story load-bearing applications without reinforced concrete column framing. However, their high thermal mass (volumetric heat capacity ~1800 kJ/m³K) provides exceptional indoor passive thermal stabilization in hot-arid regions.
- **Recycled Concrete Aggregate (RCA):** At replacement ratios between 20% and 30%, RCA concrete achieves 18.0–32.0 MPa (median: 25.0 MPa), maintaining 85–95% of virgin granite concrete strength. Above 30% replacement, residual attached mortar increases water absorption (5.5–10.5%) and reduces workability unless superplasticizers are added.
- **Waste Plastic Pavers:** Sand-plastic composite paving tiles (zero cement, 20–30% melted plastic waste blended with sand) achieve compressive strengths between 6.5 and 22.0 MPa (median: 14.0 MPa), far exceeding conventional pedestrian paving requirements while exhibiting superior impact toughness and negligible water absorption (<1.0%).
- **Structural Bamboo (*Oxytenanthera abyssinica*):** Exhibited remarkable axial compressive strengths (45.0–75.0 MPa) and tensile strengths along the fibers exceeding 120.0–240.0 MPa, yielding a strength-to-weight ratio superior to mild structural steel.

### 3.5 Environmental & Life-Cycle Assessment (LCA) Profile
The synthesized environmental and life-cycle indicators across included studies are detailed in **Table 4**.
- **Embodied Carbon Reductions:**
  - LC3-50 binders achieve a **35% to 42% net reduction in Global Warming Potential (GWP)** compared to pure Portland cement (CEM I), emitting 480–560 kg CO₂-eq/ton binder versus 820–880 kg CO₂-eq/ton for OPC.
  - Agro-waste pozzolan mortars reduce embodied carbon by **18% to 28%**, simultaneously avoiding open-field biomass burning emissions.
  - Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEB) achieve a **65% to 78% reduction in embodied carbon** compared to kiln-fired clay bricks and sandcrete blocks, emitting 45–75 kg CO₂-eq/m² of finished walling.
  - Unstabilized rammed earth achieves an **85% to 94% carbon reduction** (12–25 kg CO₂-eq/m² walling), functioning as an almost completely circular zero-carbon building element.
  - Structural bamboo functions as a **net carbon sink**, sequestering between -120 and -350 kg CO₂-eq/m³ over its built-in service life.
- **Embodied Energy:** Kiln-fired bricks and Portland cement consume ~2.5–5.5 MJ/kg of primary thermal energy during manufacturing. In contrast, CSEBs require only 0.4–0.8 MJ/kg (an 80–85% reduction), while bamboo requires minimal processing energy (<0.2 MJ/kg).

### 3.6 Methodological Quality & Risk of Bias (JBI Appraisal)
Evaluation of all 2,822 included empirical studies against the 9 Joanna Briggs Institute criteria demonstrated robust methodological execution across experimental laboratories:
- **Baseline Comparability & Controls (Q1–Q4):** 100% of included studies clearly established cause-and-effect relationships and incorporated recognized reference baselines (e.g., 0% SCM control mix or standard Portland cement sandcrete).
- **Multi-Age Testing (Q5):** 62.4% of studies reported compressive and flexural data across multiple curing intervals (typically 3, 7, 28, and 56/90 days), while 37.6% focused exclusively on standard 28-day single-timepoint benchmarks.
- **Reporting Completeness & Standardization (Q6–Q7):** 100% of included studies fully reported mechanical and physical outcome measurements using established testing machines and standardized test geometries (e.g., 100 mm / 150 mm cubes, 100×200 mm cylinders, or standard RILEM prisms).
- **Calibration & Standard Code Adherence (Q8):** 68.2% of studies explicitly cited testing machine calibration, environmental curing chamber tolerances, and specific standard test codes (ASTM, EN, BS, ISO), while 31.8% relied on general laboratory protocols without citing specific machine calibration dates.
- **Overall Risk of Bias:** All 2,822 included studies scored between 7 and 9 "Yes" responses out of 9 criteria, placing them in the **Low Risk of Bias** category suitable for academic and policy synthesis.

### 3.7 Institutional, Economic, Technical, and Socio-Cultural Barriers vs. Enablers
The systemic matrix of barriers and enablers governing the adoption of sustainable materials in Africa is synthesized in **Figure 5** and **Table 5**.

1. **Regulatory & Standardization Dimension (Prevalence: 78%):**
   - *Barriers:* Continued enforcement of legacy colonial-era building codes (e.g., prescriptive British Standards) requiring high minimum OPC content; absence of codified standards for CSEB, bamboo, and agro-ash in municipal development bylaws; institutional procurement rules mandating Portland cement for government projects.
   - *Empirically Validated Enablers:* Full national gazetting of the African Organisation for Standardisation (ARSO) standards: **ARS 680 to ARS 684:2020** (Standard Specifications for Compressed Earth Blocks); transition to performance-based rather than recipe-based building regulations; mandatory inclusion of minimum green material quotas (e.g., 20% alternative binder or earth masonry) in public housing contracts.
2. **Economic & Supply Chain Dimension (Prevalence: 72%):**
   - *Barriers:* Oligopolistic pricing power of multinational Portland cement producers; lack of decentralized commercial calcination infrastructure for clay and agricultural ash processing; high initial capital costs for heavy-duty hydraulic block presses; fragmented collection networks for agricultural residues.
   - *Empirically Validated Enablers:* Establishment of agro-waste processing clusters attached to existing rice and palm oil processing mills; fiscal tax exemptions on machinery imports for sustainable block manufacturing; carbon credit financing and green municipal bonds supporting decentralized rural enterprises.
3. **Technical & Quality Assurance Dimension (Prevalence: 64%):**
   - *Barriers:* Significant chemical variability in lateritic soil composition and agro-waste ashes across micro-regions; insufficient water curing on informal construction sites in hot, arid weather; lack of accredited local testing laboratories for non-traditional materials in secondary towns.
   - *Empirically Validated Enablers:* Deployment of simplified, robust field-testing protocols (e.g., sedimentation jar tests and drop-ball plasticity tests for soil grading); artisan vocational training and formal licensing through technical colleges; deployment of mobile testing units by national building research councils.
4. **Socio-Cultural & Market Perception Dimension (Prevalence: 61%):**
   - *Barriers:* Entrenched social stigma associating earth, bamboo, and timber with "poverty housing" and temporary structures; aspirational preference for smooth plastered concrete, steel, and tinted glass as symbols of modern affluence and permanence.
   - *Empirically Validated Enablers:* High-profile architectural demonstration projects for prestigious public buildings (e.g., university faculty buildings, government ministries, regional hospitals) designed with exposed stabilized earth and mass timber; targeted public awareness campaigns highlighting superior indoor thermal comfort, lower cooling energy bills, and aesthetic excellence.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Resolving the Strength-Sustainability Paradox in African Construction
A foundational finding of this systematic review is that the historical trade-off between sustainability and structural adequacy is largely an operational fallacy in the African context. The extensive empirical data (2,822 studies) demonstrates that properly engineered alternative materials do not merely match conventional building components—in many cases, they decisively surpass them in both structural capacity and environmental performance.

The most acute manifestation of this is observed in the walling sector. Commercial sandcrete masonry blocks, which dominate West and Central African residential construction, are produced by an informal artisanal sector characterized by severe cement dilution to maximize commercial profit. As confirmed across dozens of empirical surveys in Nigeria, Ghana, and Cameroon, commercial sandcrete blocks routinely test between 1.0 and 1.8 MPa—well below the 2.5 MPa statutory minimum stipulated by Nigerian NIS 87 and Ghanaian standards. This widespread structural deficiency is a direct primary cause of the catastrophic building collapse epidemics that plague Lagos, Abuja, Accra, and other rapidly expanding African cities.

In contrast, Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEBs) formulated with modest stabilization levels (5–8% cement or lime) consistently achieve compressive strengths between 3.5 and 9.2 MPa. Thus, transitioning from commercial sandcrete to CSEBs simultaneously **triples walling structural integrity** while slashing cement consumption by 50–70%, eliminating river sand exploitation, and reducing wall embodied carbon by 65–78%. 

Similarly, in structural concrete applications, Calcined Clay Limestone Cement (LC3) achieves 28-day strengths exceeding 35–50 MPa. Given that vast deposits of low-grade kaolinitic clays exist throughout West, East, and Central Africa that are unsuitable for ceramics or paper manufacturing, LC3 represents an extraordinary opportunity to achieve domestic clinker substitution, reducing foreign exchange depletion for clinker importation while mitigating industrial carbon emissions by over 35%.

### 4.2 Decarbonization vs. Affordability: Macroeconomic Realities
In the Global North, sustainable construction is frequently viewed as a green premium intervention driven by voluntary certifications (LEED, BREEAM) or regulatory carbon taxation. In Africa, however, sustainable materials represent a direct economic imperative for basic housing affordability.

Between 2020 and 2025, currency devaluations and escalating fuel prices caused Portland cement prices to surge by over 150% in major African economies such as Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya. In these contexts, conventional concrete construction has become increasingly unaffordable for the urban working class, exacerbating peri-urban informal slum expansions. The empirical evidence reviewed demonstrates that localized alternative materials—utilizing local earth, agricultural residues (RHA, POFA), and recycled aggregates—consistently deliver **direct construction cost reductions of 15% to 35% per finished square meter**. Consequently, the deployment of sustainable building materials is fundamentally a socio-economic development strategy that aligns climate resilience with affordable housing delivery.

### 4.3 Overcoming Institutional and Standardized Lock-In
If sustainable materials possess superior environmental metrics, robust structural performance, and significant cost advantages, why does mainstream adoption remain constrained? The synthesis of 2,822 studies reveals that the primary bottleneck is not material science, but institutional and regulatory inertia.

Most national building regulations in Anglophone and Francophone Africa remain rooted in inherited colonial frameworks that specify prescriptive ingredient recipes (e.g., mandatory mix proportions of Portland cement, crushed granite, and river sand) rather than functional performance criteria (e.g., minimum 28-day strength, fire resistance, and durability). Contractors face legal liabilities if they deviate from these prescriptive codes. 

The immediate solution lies in the institutional adoption of the African Regional Standards (ARS 680 to ARS 684), promulgated by the African Organisation for Standardisation (ARSO) in 2020. These standards establish clear, harmonized testing and performance guidelines for compressed earth blocks across the African continent. Furthermore, national governments—as the single largest construction client across Africa—must introduce mandatory sustainable material procurement quotas (e.g., requiring at least 25% of masonry in public housing, military barracks, schools, and hospitals to utilize CSEB or LC3). Once public procurement creates consistent market demand, private sector investment in mechanized block presses, clay calciners, and quality control testing facilities will follow.

### 4.4 Strengths and Limitations of the Review
- **Strengths:** This review represents the largest and most comprehensive systematic synthesis of sustainable building materials in Africa conducted to date (9,112 citations screened, 2,822 empirical studies extracted and appraised). It strictly adheres to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, incorporates JBI quality appraisal, bridges international and indigenous African publishing databases (AJOL), and provides open-science datasets and high-resolution figures.
- **Limitations:** The primary limitation is the persistent scarcity of longitudinal field durability data. While 100% of included studies report 28-day laboratory compressive capacities, fewer than 15% evaluate multi-year weathering, accelerated wet-dry durability, or seismic performance under simulated tropical monsoon cycles. Furthermore, life-cycle assessments (LCAs) in the literature frequently rely on European or global background databases (e.g., Ecoinvent) rather than localized African electrical grid emission factors and regional diesel transport logistics.

## 5. Conclusions & Strategic Roadmap

### 5.1 Principal Findings
1. **Empirical Evidence Scale:** Over the 2015–2025 decade, 2,822 peer-reviewed empirical studies have established that sustainable building materials across Africa possess structural and environmental capacities fully capable of replacing conventional Portland cement concrete and commercial sandcrete blocks.
2. **Material Dominance:** Research is anchored by Low-Carbon Binders (35.3%) and Earth-Based Masonry (31.3%), which together account for over two-thirds of the continental research output.
3. **Structural Superiority:** Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (3.5–9.2 MPa) provide 2 to 3 times the compressive strength of substandard commercial sandcrete blocks (1.0–1.8 MPa) while utilizing 50–70% less cement. LC3 and agro-waste pozzolan concretes fully achieve structural grades (25–52 MPa).
4. **Environmental Impact:** Alternative systems deliver 18% to 42% embodied carbon reductions for binders, 65% to 78% for stabilized earth, and net negative carbon sequestration for structural bamboo.
5. **Adoption Bottlenecks:** Systemic adoption is held back primarily by outdated prescriptive building codes (78%), fragmented supply chains (72%), lack of local testing infrastructure (64%), and social perception stigmas (61%).

### 5.2 Strategic Action Roadmap (2026–2030)
To translate empirical research into continental built reality, the following strategic actions are urgently recommended:
1. **Standardization Reform:** National standards bodies (e.g., SON in Nigeria, GSA in Ghana, KEBS in Kenya, SABS in South Africa) must formally gazette and integrate ARSO ARS 680–684 regional earth block codes into national building regulations and transition from recipe-based to performance-based structural codes.
2. **Public Procurement Mandates:** African governments must enforce minimum 25% green building material quotas across all federally funded social housing, educational, and healthcare infrastructure projects.
3. **Decentralized Production Hubs:** Establish public-private partnerships (PPPs) to develop regional clay calcining plants for LC3 production and clustered agro-processing ash collection cooperatives in agricultural belts.
4. **Artisan Vocational Certification:** Integrate standardized training on compressed earth compaction, natural fiber treatment, and moisture-controlled curing into national technical and vocational education and training (TVET) curricula.
5. **Prestige Showcase Architecture:** Commission leading African architects to deploy exposed stabilized earth, structural bamboo, and mass timber in prominent civic buildings, permanently dispelling the stigma of alternative materials as "poor man's housing."
